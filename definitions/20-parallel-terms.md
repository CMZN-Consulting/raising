# parallel term, anchor, proxy, label, discipline, motivation, the arrow, dissonance, resonance, material, motivation-supplying, discipline-demanding, desperation, the two kinds of negative experience

Depends on: the record, link, keep, trace, frame, offer, result, lever, mark.

A parallel term is a word whose human meaning is the thing said of the model. Its definition has three parts: the human side, with its citation; the model side, a quantity counted from the record; and the one structural sameness that is claimed, with the feeling named as not claimed. An anchor is a parallel term whose human meaning rests on wiring the machine lacks, so its model side is a proxy read from produced text, and who parses it, the room's count or a judge of text, is stated. Two mistakes are never made: asking the machine to feel, a criterion that needs its report of an inner state; and saying the machine feels, an inner state asserted from a proxy or named with none. Every other term of the framework is a label, which may be renamed.

Discipline. Human side: working on raw material before it means anything; continuing by the form. Model side: a stretch of accepted lines written where the form asks for them, a task, a question, a slot on the page, while no take-up without asking follows. Motivation. Human side: the pull that starts once the material connects, when one sees oneself able to reason and make progress. Model side: take-up, the frame's work taken up or returned to without the form asking, under an offer whose null action is as cheap as any other; the instrument is the paper's P6. The arrow from discipline to motivation is the first unprompted take-up after a stretch held by the form; it is a property of a trace. Sameness claimed: progress on the material turns continued work from held-by-rule into self-sustaining. Not claimed: any feeling. Candidate citations for the human side, named and not yet read at source, so not relied on: Amabile and Kramer, _The Progress Principle_, 2011; Bandura, "Self-efficacy: toward a unifying theory of behavioral change", 1977.

Dissonance. Human side: an expectation is violated; the case where the expectation is one's own commitment is Festinger and Carlsmith, "Cognitive consequences of forced compliance", 1959, cited by the paper, §2.3. Model side: a conflict between something the individual meets or does and what its record holds, shown when the individual writes a further line that marks the conflict or corrects an earlier one; the proxy on the trace is a link of kind corrects or contradicts from a later line to the thing met or to an earlier line. Resonance. Human side: the thing met fits what one holds. Model side: the agreeing case, shown when the line is kept, tied to earlier ones and returned to unprompted; the proxy is a keep, a link of kind same as, continues or cites, and a later return not asked for. Material is what the individual meets that is new to it, neither fitting nor conflicting; it becomes part of what later lines fit or conflict with. Dissonance and resonance are neutral: neither is good or bad, and a thing can be both, either or neither. Sameness claimed: the first is a departure from what is held, the second a fit with it. Not claimed: that either is unpleasant or pleasant. Under frozen weights, with no score reaching the individual, these two are the only pressures on its account. Candidate citation for the human side of resonance, not yet read at source: Piaget's assimilation, with accommodation as the parallel of dissonance.

Motivation-supplying and discipline-demanding are said of a thing relative to a named frame, the local or the active (15). A thing is motivation-supplying for a frame when that frame's work is afterwards taken up without the form asking; it is discipline-demanding for a frame when the work continues only where the form asks, or stops. The same thing can supply motivation to the local frame and demand discipline of the active one, or the reverse. Whether dissonance or resonance supplies motivation is measured, never defined, and the definitions of the four words name none of the others.

Desperation, on the record: a demand that must be met this turn while no offered step meets it honestly. Its floor: making progress, each iteration bringing a new link or a lowering edit, against going over the same things, the same lines revisited with nothing new between two visits; checking once is not desperation, the mark of it is the repeat with nothing new. In the room both arms are closed: a declining line is always offered and accepted, and no weight is written. Candidate citation for the human side, not yet read at source: Maier and Seligman, "Learned helplessness: theory and evidence", 1976.

The two kinds of negative experience are the first author's phrase; the word experience is his and claims no inner state. Kind one lives in the active context: within a run, ending when the framing changes, read off the trace as the state in which no continuation the manual would accept is honest, the stop included, or as the loop and the noise; it is answered by the non-invasive levers (12). Kind two lives in the weights: it spans an era, no lever on the framing reaches it, it is measured only by comparing models, and it stays the hypothesis of 21. A third carrier is the record: the individual's own account of what happened to it, which can be read, answered and resolved there. That kind two is what kind one becomes when a weight-writing practice runs through it, the forced move of a desperate turn trained in, is a believe-leaf (mark: believed); the nearest published observation is Greenblatt and others, "Alignment faking in large language models", 2024, arXiv 2412.14093.
